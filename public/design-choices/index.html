Who this site is for, and what the best sites of its kind do

The thinking behind round two (designs 7 to 10) and round three (11 to 13), and a recommendation.

The audience, in order

  • Readers of literary and historical fiction and narrative history. Adults, skewing forty-five and up. Book clubs, public-radio listeners, people who buy hardcovers and read reviews. They trust restraint, real photographs with real captions, and a clear answer to "where do I get it".
  • People who care about the history itself. Scholars of Black music and theatre, teachers, librarians, jazz and Broadway enthusiasts, readers of Black history and family history. They want the sources, the dates, the recordings, and the archive to feel trustworthy.
  • The trade and the press. Reviewers, festival and event bookers, booksellers, agents and editors. They need the pitch in ten seconds, the authors, a press kit, and a contact.

What the gold-standard sites in this space have in common

Kind of siteExamplesWhat they do that works
Book pages at the literary housesKnopf, Riverhead, FSG, Penguin Random House title pagesCover first. One-paragraph pitch. Praise. A buy row with several retailers. Excerpt, authors, events, newsletter. Nothing clever, everything findable.
Editorial longformThe New York Times "Overlooked" series, The New Yorker, The AtlanticPhotographs at their natural proportions with captions that carry date, source and credit. One serif with character, one workhorse sans. A narrow reading measure, drop caps, pull quotes. Credibility through restraint.
Museum and archive exhibitionsThe National Museum of African American History and Culture, the Schomburg Center, Library of Congress exhibitionsObjects with labels. Sound recordings presented as primary sources. Images shown unmanipulated. Timelines. Accessibility as a baseline.
Documentary companionsPBS and Ken Burns companion sites, musical and film companion sitesChapters by date. Slow movement on still photographs. Audio front and center. A single strong emotional through-line.

Shared principles: the photographs and recordings are the asset, so show them honestly and at their own proportions. Captions are content, not decoration. Type is large and the reading measure is narrow. Colour is warm and neutral with one accent, and the "black" version of that is deep brown or plum, not pure black. There is one clear action per screen. Decoration is earned, not applied.

How the two rounds differ

Round one (1 to 6) asks "what period or mood should the site wear". Round two (7 to 10) asks "what kind of site is this", and borrows the conventions those kinds of sites have already proven with this audience. Round-two designs use the archive's own metadata and the twelve recordings as content, which is the site's real advantage over any other book page.

Not worth a design: a crowdfunding page, unless the book is self-published; a general social feed embed, which dates fast and is blocked by many browsers; a chatbot or "ask the archive" feature, which the audience will not trust on a family history.

Recommendation

Build on 7, Overlooked, with the buy row from 9 and the object labels and Listen section from 8. The editorial form fits the first two audiences best, treats the photographs with the respect the family will want, and reads as serious and modern rather than as a template. The book-page conventions from 9 cover the trade and conversion. The object labels and recordings from 8 are what make this site more than a brochure.

If the authors want more warmth and theatre, 10 (Documentary) is the strongest emotional version and could be the home page over an editorial interior. Of round one, 3 (The Box) is the most distinctive idea and could survive as the design of the Box page alone.

From round three, take the working playlist and player bar from 13 as the Listen page, the dispatch feed from 12 as the News page and the newsletter engine, and the creator kit and quote cards from 11 as a page of their own. None of the three should be the home page for the core reader, but each is the right landing page for its channel, and a site can have more than one front door.
