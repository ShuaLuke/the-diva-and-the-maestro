What the website is for, what the authors still need to supply, and what we plan to add next. The site itself now documents its own structure, so this page only covers what isn't built yet.

What the site is for

One site, three readers. The home page is built for the first; the rest of the site serves all three.

  • The reader deciding whether to buy. The story in ten seconds, proof it's good in one minute, and a buy button that works. The prologue is the proof.
  • The reader with the book open. Faces, songs, and a quick answer to "who was Diamond Jim?"
  • The teacher, scholar, or descendant. The archive. This is the part of the site that will still be visited in twenty years.

How it presents the book

  • A novel based on a true story, said plainly and early. That separates it from Marva Griffin Carter's Swing Along, the academic Cook biography, and promises scenes and feeling a biography can't.
  • The box comes first. Hundreds of pages found after Mercer Cook's death is the site's origin story and its most shareable fact.
  • Abbie first, as in the title. Her page precedes his, her voice opens the home page.
  • As frank as the book. Abbie was fourteen at the audition. The site neither hides that nor sensationalizes it, and it should use the authors' own sentence on the subject.

What the authors still need to supply

Everything on the site marked "to come" or "placeholder" is waiting on one of these.

  1. Publisher, release date, and retailer links, so the button can say Buy or Pre-order and go somewhere.
  2. The cover at full resolution, and the two best portraits for the home page if the family has better ones than the public-domain pair now in use.
  3. The book's biographic summaries for Abbie, Will Marion, the historic characters, and the Cook family. They replace the placeholder text on the Diva, Maestro, and People pages.
  4. The sentence about Abbie's age, in the book's voice, for the Diva page.
  5. Author photographs and one paragraph each.
  6. The first batch of family scans: even five photographs and two scores, each with a date (or "circa"), who is in it, and where it came from.
  7. Photographs of the box and a few of the pages inside it, for The Box page.
  8. A contact address for the Authors page, and a decision on a mailing service for the newsletter form.

Also: the table of contents reads "Flies the Coup" for chapter 15. The idiom is "coop." Worth catching before it appears in print.

What to add next

Four phases, in order of payoff. None of them change the look of the site; the colors and type stay as they are until we decide otherwise.

1Sound

Hear the music where the story mentions it

The book is about two musicians and the site is nearly silent. Public-domain recordings from 1900 to 1927 exist for several Cook songs at the Library of Congress National Jukebox and archive.org, including Sousa's Band playing a Clorindy number in 1900 and Jim Europe's singers in 1919. Recordings made after 1925 are still under copyright, so later favorites like Bix Beiderbecke's "I'm Coming Virginia" can only be linked, not hosted.

Plan: verify and collect the recordings, add each to the archive with source and rights, then put a small play button in the margin of the prologue at the moment Cook plays "Darktown" and "Love in a Cottage." This is the change that makes the site feel like something other than a book page.

Status, September 5: twelve recordings found, verified public domain, and added to the archive with sources. The home page and archive now have players. Still to do in this phase: the play buttons inside the prologue, which wait on the first decision below.

2Pictures

Moving pictures, and scores you can turn

Edison and Biograph filmed cakewalks in 1903, the year of In Dahomey, and those films are public domain. Thirty seconds of footage on the In Dahomey and timeline pages would do more than any still photograph.

In the same phase, a page-turning viewer for the full NYPL scans of the two Clorindy scores, with the recording playing alongside. The archive only shows the covers now.

Status, September 5: two Biograph cakewalk films from 1903 found at the Library of Congress, trimmed of their modern leader, and added to the archive with story notes. They appear on the home page, the archive, the 1903 timeline entry, and beside the In Dahomey photographs. Still to do in this phase: the page-turning score viewer.

3Showpiece

Abbie's walk

The prologue opens with a twenty-five-block walk from Greenwich Village to the Casino roof. A scroll-driven page traces the walk on an 1890s NYPL street map with the prologue text alongside. The most work of the four, and the thing people will share.

4Family

The family's own material

The box as an object: photographs of it and of typewritten pages, with the novel's passage beside the source page it came from. A family tree from John Hartwell Cook down to Jacques and Toni. Jacques reading the preface aloud, if he's willing. None of this can be sourced from the internet, so it waits on the co-authors.

Small polish, any time

  • Reading time and a progress bar on the prologue.
  • "Then and now" photographs: the Casino Theatre was demolished in 1930; Strivers' Row still stands.
  • Chapter ornaments and a numbered contents list in the style of a printed score.
  • A custom domain. The title order is Diva first, so the domain should be too.

Decisions to make before Phase 1

  • Inline audio in the prologue, or keep the reading experience uninterrupted and the music in the archive only?
  • Recordings by white dialect singers. No Bert Williams recordings of Cook songs turned up, but several of the finds are by Billy Murray and Arthur Collins, white singers who specialized in dialect numbers, and one is from a minstrel-show disc. They are on the site now with candid captions. The authors should decide whether they stay.
  • The repository is public. The site's pages are encrypted, but the source text and images are visible on GitHub to anyone who finds it. If that becomes a concern, the options are GitHub Pro at about four dollars a month or moving hosting to Vercel.
